# The Respiratory System

## Overview

The respiratory system delivers oxygen to the bloodstream and removes carbon dioxide, the primary waste gas of cellular metabolism. It also plays roles in speech production, acid-base balance, and protecting against inhaled pathogens.

## Structures of the Respiratory System

### Upper Respiratory Tract

- **Nose and nasal cavity**: Filters, warms, and humidifies incoming air. Cilia and mucus trap particles and pathogens.
- **Sinuses**: Air-filled cavities in the skull bones; reduce skull weight and warm air.
- **Pharynx (throat)**: Common pathway for air and food; connects nasal and oral cavities to the larynx.
- **Larynx (voice box)**: Contains vocal cords; produces sound during exhalation. The epiglottis covers the larynx during swallowing to prevent food from entering the airway.

### Lower Respiratory Tract

- **Trachea (windpipe)**: Cartilaginous tube approximately 10–12 cm long connecting larynx to bronchi. Lined with cilia and mucus to trap particles.
- **Bronchi**: The trachea divides at the carina into left and right primary bronchi, each leading to a lung.
- **Bronchioles**: Smaller airways branching from bronchi; no cartilage.
- **Alveoli**: Tiny air sacs at the end of bronchioles where gas exchange occurs. The lungs contain approximately 480 million alveoli, providing a surface area of approximately 70 square metres.

### Lungs

The two lungs sit in the thoracic cavity protected by the ribcage. The right lung has three lobes (upper, middle, lower); the left lung has two lobes (upper and lower) to accommodate the heart. The lungs are covered by a two-layer membrane called the pleura.

## Breathing Mechanics

Breathing is controlled by the respiratory centre in the medulla oblongata, primarily responding to blood CO₂ levels.

**Inhalation**: The diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle below the lungs) contracts and flattens; the intercostal muscles between the ribs contract to expand the chest; lung volume increases; air flows in.

**Exhalation**: The diaphragm relaxes and domes upward; chest volume decreases; air flows out. At rest, exhalation is passive.

## Gas Exchange

In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses from inhaled air into capillary blood, binding to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli and is exhaled. This exchange is driven by differences in partial pressure across the thin alveolar-capillary membrane.

## Respiratory Volumes

- **Tidal volume**: Air inhaled or exhaled in a normal breath (~500 mL).
- **Vital capacity**: Maximum air that can be exhaled after a maximal breath (~4,800 mL).
- **Residual volume**: Air remaining in lungs after maximal exhalation (~1,200 mL).

# The Digestive System

## Overview

The digestive system breaks down food into nutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water) that the body can absorb and use for energy, growth, and repair.

## Structures of the Digestive System

### Oral Cavity

Digestion begins in the mouth. Teeth mechanically break down food; saliva moistens food and contains amylase, which begins starch digestion.

### Pharynx and Oesophagus

The pharynx transfers food from the mouth to the oesophagus. The oesophagus is a muscular tube approximately 25 cm long connecting the throat to the stomach. Peristalsis (rhythmic wave-like muscle contractions) propels food downward.

### Stomach

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular organ in the upper left abdomen. It stores food (capacity approximately 1 litre), mechanically churns it, and secretes gastric acid (HCl) and enzymes (pepsin) to begin protein digestion. The stomach produces chyme, a semi-liquid mixture, which passes into the small intestine via the pyloric sphincter.

### Small Intestine

The small intestine is approximately 6–7 metres long and divided into three sections:
- **Duodenum**: First 25 cm. Receives chyme, bile from the liver (via the bile duct), and pancreatic enzymes. Most chemical digestion occurs here.
- **Jejunum**: Middle section (~2.5 m). Major site of nutrient absorption.
- **Ileum**: Final section (~3.5 m). Absorbs remaining nutrients, particularly vitamin B12 and bile acids.

The inner wall is lined with villi and microvilli, dramatically increasing surface area for absorption.

### Large Intestine (Colon)

Approximately 1.5 metres long. Absorbs water and electrolytes from undigested material, forming faeces. Houses trillions of beneficial gut bacteria (microbiome) that ferment fibre and produce vitamin K. Divided into caecum, ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon, and sigmoid colon.

### Rectum and Anus

The rectum stores faeces until defaecation. The internal and external anal sphincters control release.

## Accessory Organs

### Liver

The liver is the largest internal organ (~1.5 kg). Functions include:
- Produces bile (stored in the gallbladder) for fat digestion.
- Processes nutrients absorbed from the intestines.
- Detoxifies alcohol, drugs, and metabolic waste.
- Produces blood proteins, cholesterol, and glycogen storage.
- Produces clotting factors.

### Pancreas

The pancreas serves both digestive (exocrine) and hormonal (endocrine) functions:
- **Exocrine**: Secretes digestive enzymes (protease, lipase, amylase) into the duodenum.
- **Endocrine**: Islets of Langerhans produce insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises blood glucose).

### Gallbladder

Stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver; releases it into the duodenum when fat is present.
